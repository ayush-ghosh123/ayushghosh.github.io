Project File // FILTER-01 · ESB Technical Design · UC Berkeley

Mechanical Emergency Water Filtration System

A purely mechanical, human-powered water filtration system for disaster relief, designed to deliver 10 L/min of clean water through a reusable 0.2 µm ceramic barrier, drawing from ambient unpressurized water with no electricity. A self-priming pump feeds a ceramic bank, a pneumatic accumulator fires reverse-flow backwash pulses so the filters are reusable rather than disposable, and an elevated tank stores potential energy to keep water flowing after power input stops. I took it end to end: first-principles analysis, five MATLAB models, full CAD, FEA, and CFD.

01
Requirements

Problem & Constraints

The brief was a mechanical water filter for disaster zones, bound by six hard constraints: a reusable 0.2 µm filter with no disposable cartridges, at least 10 L/min of throughput, operation on ambient unpressurized water, purely mechanical operation with no electricity, semi-automatic behavior that keeps dispensing for 3–5 minutes after human power stops, and a power-input-agnostic drive shaft with one-way transmission so it couples to any rotating source.

On top of those I imposed my own criteria, portability, low maintenance, low cost, and ease of manufacture, and framed the design around two stated assumptions: trained relief operators are present and supervising, and chemical post-treatment follows storage. Those assumptions matter because they bound what the mechanical system has to do on its own.

02
Ideation · Architecture

Concept Development & Final Schematic

I iterated through three architectures. The first concept ran a double-acting piston pump through a pre-filter and primary filter into a pneumatic accumulator, but the piston pump needed a linkage to convert rotary input into reciprocating motion. The second explored a rotary ceramic crossflow arrangement with an elevated tank, which is elegantly self-cleaning but demands motor-level rpm that blows the human-power budget.

The final architecture is dead-end filtration with accumulator backwash: a mesh strainer and pre-filter protect a five-element ceramic bank, a self-priming flexible-impeller pump drives the flow, a nitrogen-charged accumulator fires reverse pulses to lift the cake off the membrane, and an elevated tank provides gravity-fed coast-down. The schematic captures the valve logic, a main valve, a dirty-line drain, and an isolation valve, that switches the system between filtration and backwash modes.

03
Analysis · MATLAB

Power Delivery

I modeled the full pressure budget in MATLAB, summing the membrane Darcy resistance, the fouling cake, the pre-filter, pipe friction via Haaland and Darcy–Weisbach, and static lift, then divided hydraulic power by pump and drivetrain efficiencies to size the required human input. The worst case is the fill-and-dispense phase on fouled filters, where the pump must feed the 10 L/min output and top up the elevated tank at the same time.

Peak input lands at ~95 W against a 200 W human budget, with steady dispensing near 50 W, comfortably feasible for a person on a crank or bicycle drive. A sensitivity sweep identified the output flow regulator as the single biggest lever on required power, and the flexible-impeller pump's ~50% efficiency as the dominant loss in the chain.

Input power & pressure budget
04
Analysis · MATLAB

Filter Sizing & Backwash Interval

Membrane area follows directly from flow divided by flux. Holding the design flux at 200 LMH, safely below the critical-flux fouling knee, gives roughly 3 m² of 0.2 µm ceramic, about six Atech elements. Modeling cake growth through Darcy's law, where resistance accumulates as the product of specific cake resistance, feed solids, flux, and time, yields the backwash trigger.

The key result is that the interval scales as 1/J²: halving the flux quadruples the run time between cleanings, so a slightly larger, gentler filter pays off far more than linearly. At the design point the system backwashes about every 11 minutes, with the pre-filter's solids load as the dominant lever on run time. The cake parameters are literature-calibrated, so this interval is an estimate I would pin down with a bench test on real source water.

05
Analysis · MATLAB

Accumulator Backwash

The backwash pulse is driven by nitrogen compressed behind a bladder, modeled with Boyle's law. Applying a never-empty rule so the bladder never fully collapses, a 12 L accumulator delivers about 3.5 L usable, roughly 30% of the shell, in a sharp ~11 second pulse.

Because instantaneous flow is proportional to the available pressure, and that pressure decays as the gas expands, the pulse is not constant, it starts strong and tapers. Solving the coupled differential equation shows the discharge volume grows as √t, confirming the decaying shape. Pressure is never the limiting factor (~0.2 bar needed to reverse-flow and lift the cake versus 1.5–3 bar available), so usable volume and duration govern, and the strong initial burst is exactly what cracks the cake loose.

06
Analysis · MATLAB

Gravity Coast-Down

To satisfy the semi-automatic requirement, the elevated tank dispenses by gravity once human power stops. I solved the draining-tank ODE, Torricelli outflow coupled to a mass balance, both numerically with RK4 and in closed form, using the agreement between the two as a validation cross-check.

A 3.6 mm orifice sustains about 2 L/min for roughly 3 minutes from the 6 L tank, and flow drifts only ~5% across the whole drain because the fixed 1.5 m static head dominates the changing water depth inside the tank. The result is a steady finishing trickle with zero moving parts and no control system, the requirement met by geometry alone.

Torricelli drainage ODE
07
Drivetrain

Power-Agnostic Drive & Coupler

The input interface is a clamp coupler paired with a one-way sprag clutch, so the device accepts any rotating power source and will not back-drive under idle input. A keyless chuck on the input stub lets a drill, crank, or bicycle drive clamp straight on, which is what makes the system genuinely power-input-agnostic.

From T = P/ω, the worst-case design torque is about 9 N·m including a 3× service factor at the lowest input speed. Checking friction-grip capacity, a single standard M4 class-12.9 clamp screw holds with roughly 4× margin, so torque capacity is never the binding constraint. The real design concerns are the friction coefficient dropping on a wet shaft and keeping water and grit out of the sprag clutch, which is why a keyed positive drive is the production refinement.

CAD

Full System Assembly

The complete system was modeled in SolidWorks on a portable, caster-mounted frame. The assembly integrates the mesh strainer, self-priming pump on its saddle mount, inline pre-filter, the horizontal ceramic housing on cradle supports, the pneumatic accumulator, the elevated tank with its float valve, inline pressure gauge, flow regulators, and the full valve network that switches between filtration and backwash.

Packaging drove real decisions: the housing runs horizontally to keep the center of gravity low, the accumulator and tank sit high so gravity does the coast-down work for free, and the drive interface is placed at the frame edge so an external power source can couple to it without reaching into the machine.

09
Component Design

Ceramic Housing Pressure Vessel

The ceramic bank lives inside a custom 6061-T6 aluminum pressure vessel, a 130 mm bore, 1.7 m long tube with bolted end caps, rated to the 3 bar relief set-point. Aluminum was chosen for the best strength-to-weight, roughly 7.7 kg versus about 20 kg for the same geometry in stainless, with hard anodizing for freshwater corrosion resistance. For saltwater deployment I would switch to 316 stainless and accept the weight penalty.

The five ceramic monoliths seat into bored pockets and seal with radial O-rings. That choice is deliberate: ceramic is brittle and cannot take a clamping load, so a radial seal is squeezed sideways by interference and energized further by system pressure, sealing with no axial force on the element. The elements are captured between two tube sheets rather than clamped, and the whole bundle lifts out as a serviceable cartridge, which is what makes the filter genuinely reusable.

Simulation · FEA

Pressure Vessel FEA

I validated the housing in SolidWorks Simulation against a hand-calculated hoop-stress check, fixing the saddle supports and applying 3 bar internal pressure to the wetted surfaces. The uniform mid-body stress field matches thin-wall theory, which is the cross-check that confirms the model is set up correctly.

Peak von Mises stress is 8.49 MPa against a 275 MPa yield, a factor of safety near 32, so the vessel is loaded to roughly 3% of yield. As expected, the peak sits at a port stress concentration where material is removed for the penetration, with a milder discontinuity stress where the flat end cap restrains the cylinder. Maximum displacement is just 0.014 mm. The honest caveat is that stress concentrations are mesh-sensitive, so a production design would get a mesh-refinement study and a port fillet, though with this much margin the conclusion holds either way.

Simulation · CFD

Backwash Flow Distribution in ANSYS Fluent

A backwash pulse is only useful if it cleans all five elements, so I ran a steady, single-phase ANSYS Fluent study of the reverse flow through the inlet diffuser cone and the five channels. The fluid volume was extracted in SpaceClaim, meshed, and solved with a velocity inlet and pressure outlet, converged to tight residuals with the inlet velocity verified by surface integral.

The cross-sectional velocity contour revealed a flow maldistribution: the top channel carries roughly 5× the velocity of the outer four, meaning the diffuser cone does not split the pulse evenly and some elements would be under-cleaned. That is a real flaw the analysis caught rather than a result that confirmed what I wanted. The near-term mitigation is firing 2–3 pulses per backwash; the proper fix is a flow-distributor plate or a longer diffuser cone, which I would re-run in CFD to verify an even split.

BOM · Manufacturing

Bill of Materials, Manufacturing & Mass

The full system comes in at roughly 62 kg and ~$2,900 in parts, built mostly from off-the-shelf components around three custom pieces: the ceramic housing, the cart frame, and the mounting brackets.

The housing is machined from 6061-T6 with bolted or tie-rod-clamped end caps, deliberately avoiding welds, since welding 6061 anneals the T6 temper and roughly halves yield strength in the heat-affected zone. The cart is bolt-together T-slot extrusion, requiring no welding or fabrication skill and staying reconfigurable through design iteration. Wetted lines are potable-rated plastic rather than metal, reinforced PVC on the suction side to resist collapse under vacuum and PEX on the pressure side, matched to the 3 bar ambient-temperature duty. The manufacturing strategy is quantity-driven: subtractive machining for a one-off, shifting to extrusion, casting, and stamping at production volume.

13
Reflection

Failure Modes & Next Steps

I built an FMEA around the highest-severity risks and paired each with a concrete validation test: bladder or pump-seal leakage causing cross-contamination, checked by a 24-hour pressure-decay hold and a dye trace; impeller wear dropping output below spec, checked by a 100-hour run on turbid water; fouling runaway that backwash cannot recover, checked by tracking peak ΔP across repeated cycles; and coupler slip on a wet shaft, checked by measuring slip torque wet versus dry.

With more time the priorities are clear. First, a bench test on real source water to replace the literature-estimated cake parameters with measured data, which is what turns the backwash interval from an estimate into a number. Second, a flow-distributor plate to resolve the CFD maldistribution so a single pulse cleans every element. Third, a differential-pressure gauge across the bank as a live clogging indicator, with the backwash trigger marked in red on the dial so a non-expert operator knows exactly when to act. Those three moves take a design that is sound on paper and make it proven in the field.

Instrumentation

Four-Point Probe Resistance

To ground the thermal work in real numbers, I measured the busbar and joint resistances directly with a four-point (Kelvin) milliohm meter. The four-point method drives a known current through one pair of leads and senses voltage across a separate pair, so it cancels out the lead and contact resistance and resolves the true milliohm-level resistance of the conductor and its joints. These measurements pinpoint where the resistive heating is actually generated and set the electrical baseline for the thermal predictions.

Four-point probe resistance test

Instrumentation

Arduino Thermocouple Thermal Rig

I built an Arduino-based measurement rig with a thermocouple sensor array to capture the busbars' real thermal behavior. A bench power supply drives representative current through the busbar network while the thermocouples log the temperature rise, and the telemetry is streamed out and visualized in Python and matplotlib. This produces a time-resolved temperature map of the conductors under load.

06
Simulation · CFD

ANSYS Coupled-Field Thermal Cross-Check

In parallel with the physical testing, I ran ANSYS coupled-field thermal simulations on the busbars to cross-check the experimental results. The model couples the electrical and thermal fields: I apply the measured current and copper's resistivity so ANSYS computes the Joule (I²R) heat generated inside the conductor, then define convective and radiative boundary conditions to the surrounding enclosure air so the solver captures how that heat is carried away. After meshing the busbar and solving for the steady-state temperature field, the simulation predicts the peak conductor temperature and shows exactly where the hotspots land along the geometry.

Because the current load and resistivity come straight from the four-point probe data, the simulated temperatures can be compared directly against the thermocouple readings, so the ANSYS model and the physical rig validate each other rather than each standing on its own. That agreement is what lets me trust the simulation when exploring geometry changes that would be slow or expensive to test physically.

Validation, Redesign & Status

That closed loop of measure, simulate, cross-check, and redesign directly improves the reliability of the accumulator power distribution system. Module Stack-Up

I designed the module as a layered sandwich built around the cells. Two module clamshells form the outer structure and carry the copper current collectors that tie the 3P groups together and route current to the next module. Inside, the cells sit in a cell holder plate backed by the polycarbonate plate, with 3D-printed standoffs setting the spacing. Because every module is identical, each design decision on the plate is multiplied by ten, which is why the plate's thickness, mass, and machinability got so much attention.

02
Material Selection

Choosing the Insulating Plate Material

The plate has to do three jobs at once: insulate a ~504 V stack, hold 42 cells through crash loads, and pass the rulebook's flammability requirement. I compared four candidates, polycarbonate, Garolite FR-4, PEI, and Garolite G-10, across dielectric strength, yield strength, cost, hardness, flammability rating, stiffness, glass transition temperature, and density.

Polycarbonate won on the criteria that mattered most here. It has the highest dielectric strength of the group (16 kV/mm), the lowest density (1.20 g/cm³), which compounds across ten modules, and it meets the UL94-V0 requirement of rule F.1.18, which G-10 (UL94-HB) does not. Its lower hardness also makes the cell counterbores far easier to machine cleanly. PEI was stronger and more heat-resistant but cost two to four times as much, and the fiberglass laminates were heavier and harder on tooling. Polycarbonate's lower yield strength (62 MPa) was the trade-off, so the mechanical sizing that follows had to prove it was enough.

Material trade study
03
Hand Calcs · Electrical

Thickness Check: Breakdown & Insulation Resistance

First I checked whether electrical requirements set a minimum thickness. For dielectric breakdown, the required thickness is t = FoS · V / E. Using a factor of safety of 2, the full 504 V pack voltage, and a conservative 15 kV/mm, that comes out to just 0.08 mm.

Next was insulation resistance. The rulebook (EV.8.6.3 and IN.10.2.2) sets a floor of 500 Ω/V, so the insulation monitoring device trips at 500 Ω/V × 504 V = 252 kΩ. The plate's resistance through its thickness is R = ρ · t / A; with polycarbonate's volume resistivity of about 10¹⁵ Ω·cm, a 0.64 cm plate, and 411 cm² of area, that is on the order of 10¹² Ω, millions of times above the trip threshold. Both checks pass with huge margin, so the conclusion was clear: thickness is not set by the electrical properties, and the mechanical loads would govern.

Breakdown voltage & insulation resistance
04
Hand Calcs · Mechanical

Thickness Check: Crash Loads

The rulebook requires the module structure to protect the cells from 40g longitudinal, 40g lateral, and 20g vertical accelerations. With 42 cells at about 70 g each, the cell mass is 2.94 kg. Applying F = m·a and spreading it over the wall area gives 1154 N (28.1 kPa) in the longitudinal and lateral cases and 577 N (14.0 kPa) vertically.

The two load cases stress the plate differently. The 20g vertical case pushes the cells out of plane, so I modeled the plate as a simply supported plate on four edges using Roark's Formulas for Stress and Strain (Table 11.4): σmax = βqb²/t², rearranged for tmin against an allowable stress of 62 MPa / 1.5 = 41.3 MPa. The 40g cases load the plate in plane, where σ = F / (w·t).

The out-of-plane case governs. It needs a minimum of 2.08 mm and sees about 17.7 MPa at the design thickness, while the 40g in-plane case needs only 0.15 mm and sees 1.9 MPa. That 2.08 mm is actually conservative, since it stacks two safety margins: the rulebook crash accelerations themselves and the 1.5 factor of safety on top. On paper that would allow stepping down to a 1/8-inch plate, but I kept the thicker plate because a thinner one would not leave enough depth for the cells to seat in their counterbores. Sometimes the governing constraint is the manufacturing feature, not the stress.

Simulation · FEA

Plate FEA Validation

To check the hand calcs, I ran a SolidWorks static study on the plate with the cell loads applied at every counterbore pocket and the edges restrained. Peak von Mises stress came out at about 20.1 MPa against polycarbonate's 62 MPa yield, a factor of safety of roughly 3.

That lands within about 14% of the 17.7 MPa hand-calc prediction, and the difference makes sense: Roark's formula treats the plate as a solid sheet, while the FEA captures the stress concentrations around the counterbores where material is removed. Getting two independent methods to agree this closely is what gives me confidence the plate is sized correctly.

Analysis · MATLAB

Fastener Sizing for Nylon Screws

We want to hold the module together with nylon slotted hex screws, which are non-conductive and light, while using as few fasteners as possible. Nylon is far weaker than steel, though, so I wrote a MATLAB sizing script to find how few screws we can get away with. The modules are not bolted to the pack casing; the casing walls hold them in place. That means the screws inside the module only carry load when a part's own inertia pulls it away from what it is attached to. The script focuses on the two groups that carry the most: the clamshell screws and the standoff and support screws.

For each group, the script adds up the mass of every part those screws hold and applies the three rulebook load cases with a 1.5 factor of safety: 20g vertical, which puts the screws in tension, and 40g longitudinal and 40g lateral, which put them in shear. It then checks every way a single screw can fail. In tension, it checks the screw breaking, the nylon threads stripping, the heat-set insert pulling out of the printed part, and the screw head pulling through the clamped plate. In shear, it checks the screw shearing and the hole bearing on the clamped part. The nylon's strength is knocked down for absorbed moisture and for cell operating temperature, and a load-share factor accounts for screws never splitting load perfectly evenly.

The script sweeps screw sizes from M3 to M8 and counts from 1 to 16, then plots the margin of safety of the most heavily loaded screw. Where each curve crosses zero is the minimum screw count for that size. The clearest result is that shear drives the count: with M4 screws, the clamshell joint needs about 4 screws for the 20g tension case but about 14 for the 40g shear cases. That points to the best way to cut fasteners: let geometric features such as pins, lips, or tongue-and-groove joints carry the shear, so the screws only need to clamp the parts together, which brings each joint down to roughly 3 to 4 screws. The script has a switch to model exactly that.

The thread-engagement sweep answered the thread-stripping question. A nylon M4 only needs about 3 mm of engagement before the screw body becomes the weak point, so the nylon threads will not strip out of an 8 mm brass insert. The real limit for larger screws in tension is the insert pulling out of the printed standoff.

01
First Principles

Propulsion & Power Hand Calculations

The whole design started on paper. I worked from the vehicle weight outward to prove, before buying a single part, that the motors, props, and battery could actually lift and sustain the aircraft.

Weight, disk area & thrust budget. Starting from an all-up mass of 0.894 kg, the weight is W = mg = 8.77 N, which the four props must support. Each 10-inch prop sweeps a disk of A = πD²/4 = 0.0507 m². With each motor producing up to ~7.85 N, total thrust is 31.4 N, giving a thrust-to-weight ratio of 3.58, comfortably above the ~2:1 minimum for controllable flight. Splitting weight across four motors, each only needs 2.19 N (~224 gf) to hover, out of ~800 gf available, so hover sits near 28% throttle, leaving plenty of headroom.

Momentum (actuator-disk) theory. Modeling each rotor as an actuator disk, I solved for the induced velocity vᵢ = √(T / 2ρA) = 4.20 m/s, then the ideal induced power Pᵢdₑₐₗ = T·vᵢ. Dividing by a figure of merit (~0.65) converts that to real shaft power: about 14.2 W per motor at hover, rising to ~96 W per motor at full throttle.

Empirical thrust model & RPM cross-check. To sanity-check the propulsion pairing, I used an empirical static-thrust model to back out the required ~7930 RPM for the target thrust, then confirmed the motor's Kv·V no-load speed (12,600 RPM) put that at a healthy 63% of no-load. A tip-speed and Mach check gave a tip velocity of 105 m/s, or Mach 0.31, safely subsonic, so no compressibility losses at the blade tips.

Power, current & endurance. Converting shaft power through the motor efficiency gives ~120 W electrical per motor, or 10.8 A each and ~43 A total at full throttle. The 3S 2200 mAh pack's discharge rating clears that draw, and using 80% usable capacity against the hover current yields an estimated endurance of about 12 minutes.

FEA Frame Validation

With the loads known, I validated the frame geometry in SolidWorks FEA, fixing the center plate and applying the motor thrust and landing loads out at the arm tips. The static stress plot showed a peak von Mises stress of about 4.31 MPa against a material yield of ~60 MPa, a factor of safety near 14×, so the frame is nowhere close to yielding. The displacement plot showed the expected behavior: the arms deflect most at their tips (peak ~10.6 mm at the exaggerated load case) while the central hub stays rigid, confirming stiffness is concentrated where the electronics and battery sit.

03
Electronics

Flight-Controller PCB in KiCad

Rather than run an off-the-shelf autopilot, I designed my own flight controller in KiCad, a single board carrying every subsystem the aircraft needs. The schematic is organized into functional blocks:

At the center is an STM32F405 (Cortex-M4F) MCU, the brain, with a 16 MHz crystal, decoupling and VCAP capacitors, an SWD programming header, and BOOT0/reset circuitry. A buck-converter block steps the 3S LiPo (~11.1–12.6 V) down to regulated 5 V and 3.3 V rails and drives a power LED. A battery screw terminal feeds the pack in, and a 33k/10k voltage divider taps it into an ADC pin so the firmware can monitor pack voltage. Four ESC signal outputs (Motor 0–3) run on 3-pin headers with 33 Ω series resistors, wired so only a single ESC's BEC feeds the 5 V rail. An ELRS RC-receiver connects over UART (with TX/RX crossed). For sensing, an MPU-6050 IMU (3-axis gyro + accelerometer, with interrupt) and a BMP280 barometer sit on the I²C buses, the IMU for attitude, the barometer for altitude. A USB-micro port with a USBLC6 ESD-protection device handles programming and telemetry, and four mounting holes tie it to the frame.

The board layout places the STM32 and crystal centrally with tight decoupling, groups the ESC headers along one edge and the sensor headers on the I²C side, isolates the buck converter and its inductor in a corner, and keeps the mounting holes on the vehicle's bolt pattern. The populated board shows the final result: the STM32 reflowed in the center, the purple BMP280 barometer and blue MPU-6050 IMU on their headers, the screw terminal for battery power, and the pin headers broken out for the four ESCs and the receiver.

04
Software

Flight Firmware From Scratch

With the board in hand, I wrote the flight firmware from the ground up on the STM32, no autopilot stack underneath it. At boot the firmware brings up the clocks, I²C, UART, timers, and ADC, then initializes and calibrates the IMU and barometer. The core is a fixed-rate control loop that runs every cycle: read the gyro/accelerometer and barometer, fuse them into an attitude and altitude estimate, read the pilot's stick commands from the ELRS receiver over UART, run the control laws, and write motor commands out to the four ESCs via timer-generated PWM. The same loop watches pack voltage through the divider and enforces failsafes, for example, cutting motors if the receiver link drops, so the aircraft fails safe rather than flying away.

05
State Estimation

Complementary-Filter Sensor Fusion

A quadcopter can't be stabilized from raw sensors: the gyroscope is fast and smooth but drifts as its bias integrates over time, while the accelerometer gives an absolute gravity reference but is noisy and corrupted by the vehicle's own vibration and motion. I implemented a complementary filter (α = 0.98) to fuse them into one clean attitude estimate: it high-pass-filters the integrated gyro rates for the fast, short-term response and low-pass-filters the accelerometer's gravity vector to correct long-term drift, with the α weighting setting how much to trust each source. The result is a stable, low-latency estimate of pitch and roll (with the barometer folded in for altitude) that holds steady even under motor vibration, the foundation the control loops depend on.

06
Controls · Bring-Up

PID Tuning on a Tethered Rig

With a trustworthy attitude estimate, the last step was closing the loop. The firmware runs PID controllers that turn the error between commanded and estimated attitude into per-motor corrections, the proportional term reacting to current error, the derivative term damping the rate of change, and the integral term trimming out steady-state offset. To tune the gains safely I built a tethered test rig, anchoring the drone to heavy dumbbells so it could spool up and react to inputs without flying away. Working axis by axis, I raised each proportional gain until the response was crisp but not oscillating, added derivative to damp overshoot, and trimmed integral to hold attitude, until the aircraft settled quickly and rejected disturbances cleanly.

Wind Turbine Design & Testing

A full-cycle mechanical engineering project, research, CAD, FEA, 3D printing, and physical testing, to design a small-scale horizontal-axis wind turbine that generates power in a 25 mph laboratory wind. The final build pairs a three-blade NACA 4412 rotor with a tapered lattice tower, and reached roughly 58.9% aerodynamic efficiency, essentially at the theoretical Betz limit of 59%.

Aerodynamics

Blade & Airfoil Design

The rotor was designed around four key parameters. The blade uses a NACA 4412 airfoil, chosen for its high lift-to-drag ratio at low wind speeds and a 12% thickness-to-chord ratio that also eases 3D printing. A three-blade layout balances smooth, low-vibration rotation against the added drag of more blades. Each blade runs a 6° angle of attack and twists from 20° at the root to 0° at the tip, holding the local angle of attack favorable along the span. Final blade length was 2.75 in from a 0.25 in hub offset.

Tower Concept & Selection

The tower carries the motor and rotor while staying light and stiff. After sketching several concepts, the team chose a tapered square-truss lattice with diagonal cross-bracing: triangulated members resist deformation far better than open rectangular frames, and the wide base lowers the center of gravity and resists overturning. Research comparing lattice and tubular towers drove the decision to use an open truss rather than a solid column.

Conclusions & Next Steps

Design for Emerging and Nanoscale Manufacturing, UC Berkeley

February 2026 - Present

This research focuses on Computed Axial Lithography (CAL), a volumetric additive manufacturing technique that fabricates three-dimensional objects by tomographically synthesizing light within a rotating volume of photopolymer precursor solution. Unlike conventional layer-by-layer 3D printing methods, CAL generates an entire object simultaneously by projecting dynamically computed light patterns into the rotating resin volume. When the integrated light dose within the resin reaches a polymerization threshold, the desired geometry solidifies throughout the volume at once, enabling extremely rapid fabrication of complex structures.

My work in the Design for Emerging and Nanoscale Manufacturing research group centers on the mechanical design of the CAL printing hardware, the motion system that spins and positions the resin volume during a print. Working in SolidWorks, I designed a lead-screw and actuator–driven mechanism that rotates the resin vial while simultaneously translating it along the z-axis. This coordinated rotation and vertical travel is what volumetric tomographic printing depends on: the vial must spin smoothly and repeatably while the projected light patterns are synchronized to its angular position, so the mechanical design has to hold precise, low-runout motion across the full travel range.

To keep the print accurate, I also developed an alignment feedback system that pairs camera-based imaging with a motorized stage. The camera images the vial and its position in real time, and the motorized stage makes closed-loop corrections to keep the vial precisely aligned to the projection axis. This registration between the optical system and the rotating resin volume is critical to print fidelity, and closing that loop in hardware directly improves the geometric accuracy of the fabricated parts.

Alongside the hardware, I contribute to the data and machine-learning side of the workflow, building a data pipeline for the volumetric printing experiments and training neural network models for the tomographic inverse problem, computing the light-dose projections needed to polymerize a target geometry within the rotating resin volume.

Computational Mechanics Research Group, University of Maryland, Baltimore County

June 2024 - August 2025

This research focused on accelerating digital image correlation (DIC), a widely used experimental mechanics technique for measuring full-field displacement and strain in deforming materials. DIC typically works by comparing images of a speckle pattern applied to a specimen before and after deformation and computing the displacement of the speckle features between the two images. While highly accurate, traditional DIC algorithms are computationally intensive and can require several hours to process large datasets.

To address this limitation, I developed a convolutional neural network (CNN) capable of predicting full-field displacement maps directly from paired speckle images representing the undeformed and deformed states of a material. The model was trained on a large dataset of synthetically generated speckle images with known displacement fields. These datasets were generated and curated in MATLAB, producing more than 10,000 training image pairs with controlled deformation patterns.

Preliminary testing suggests that the neural network can reproduce displacement fields with high fidelity while reducing computation time by approximately 98 percent, decreasing processing time from roughly six hours to around six minutes for comparable analyses. The model is currently undergoing further validation by researchers in the Computational Mechanics Research Group to evaluate its performance across a broader range of deformation conditions and experimental datasets.
